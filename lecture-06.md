```text
Farkas lemma
Theorem (Farkas Lemma)
Let A be an m × n matrix, b ∈Rm, and F = {x ∈Rn : Ax = b, x ≥0}. Then either F ̸= ∅
or there exists y ∈Rm such that yA ≥0 and y · b < 0 but not both.
It is useful to rephrase Farkas lemma in the following way:
Either the system of linear equations Ax = b has a non-negative solution, or the system
yA ≥0, y · b < 0 has a solution, but not both.
The system yA ≥0 and y · b < 0 is sometimes referred to as the Farkas alternative.
```

```text
Theorem (Farkas Lemma)
Let A be an m × n matrix, b ∈Rm, and F = {x ∈Rn : Ax = b, x ≥0}. Then either F ̸= ∅
or there exists y ∈Rm such that yA ≥0 and y · b < 0, but not both.
(Algebraic) intuition:
Take any linear combination of the equations in Ax = b to get yAx = y · b.
Suppose the first system has non-negative solution, then it must also be a solution to the
second.
If we can choose y so that yA ≥0 and y · b < 0, we find that the left-hand side of the
single equation yAx = y · b is at least zero while the right-hand side is strictly negative, a
contradiction.
Thus, the first system cannot have a non-negative solution.
```

```text
Application: Arbitrage
Arbitrage is the practice of taking advantage of a difference in prices in two or more markets.
In the long run, arbitrage opportunities shouldn’t prevail: investors take advantages of
such opportunities until the prices are adjusted and they disappear.
In other words, arbitrage opportunities can only exist for an extremely brief period of
time: Almost as soon as they are discovered, they are wiped out as a result of the
response of investors.
The absence of arbitrage opportunities is the driving principle of financial theory.
In economics and finance, we say that an arbitrage opportunity exists if there exists an
investment portfolio that pays off non-negative amounts in every scenario that has a strictly
negative cost.
```

```text
The discussion above gives rise to the following famous result in finance.
Theorem (Arbitrage Theorem)
Let A be an m × n payoff matrix, and let p denote the vector of (relative) asset prices. Exactly
one of the following statements holds:
(a) There exists an investment portfolio y ∈Rm such that yA ≥0 and y · p < 0.
(b) There is a probability vector π ∈Rn
+ (with ∑︁n
j=1 πj = 1) such that p = Aπ.
Intuitively, the theorem states that
either the expected value of all possible assets is the same and equal to their prices,
or it is possible to create an arbitrage portfolio at a strictly negative cost that guarantees
a non-negative return for every possible scenario,
but not both. In other words, no arbitrage opportunities implies that market is “fair” in the
sense that all assets are fairly priced.
```
